Overview

In this homework we implemented a path tracer. For Part 1, we generated rays from the camera and implemented intersection tests for triangles and spheres. In Part 2 we built a BVH to make ray intersection faster, since checking every triangle becomes really slow for bigger scenes. We then implemented direct lighting in Part 3 and indirect lighting in Part 4, where we trace additional bounces to get global illumination. Finally, Part 5 implements adaptive sampling, so we don't need to use the same number of samples for every pixel. From the results, the BVH made a big difference for large meshes (around 6,800× for Lucy), and adding more bounces and samples helped us render the lighting more realistically.

Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Ray generation and the rendering pipeline

To render each pixel we take several samples at random positions inside the pixel and average their colors. For each sample we add a random offset in \([0,1)^2\) to the pixel's bottom left corner. After this we divide by the width and height of the image to convert the coordinates appropriately. We then pass this to the camera to generate a ray. We trace the ray and get its radiance. Averaging all of these samples gives us the final pixel color.

For ray generation we use camera space with the camera at the origin looking in the \(-z\) direction. The sensor plane is at \(z=-1\). From the horizontal and vertical field of view we calculate the half width and height of the sensor:

\[ w = \tan\left(\frac{hFov}{2}\right), \qquad h = \tan\left(\frac{vFov}{2}\right). \]

Since \((0,0)\) represents the bottom left of the sensor and \((1,1)\) the top right, the position corresponding to our sample is:

\[ \mathbf{p}_{cam} = \big( (2x - 1)\, w,\; (2y - 1)\, h,\; -1 \big). \]

We use this position as the direction of the ray in camera space since the camera is at the origin. We then transform it into world space using the camera-to-world matrix and normalize the direction.

Primitive intersection

For each primitive, we implemented two intersection functions. One function only returns whether the ray intersects the primitive, which we use for shadow rays. The other function also stores the intersection information such as \(t\), the normal and the material. We check that the intersection is between the minimum and maximum \(t\) of the ray. When we find a valid hit, we update the maximum \(t\) to the new value. Thus, later intersections only count if they are closer than the previous one, so we end up with the closest intersection.

Triangles. For triangles, the Moller Trumbore algorithm was used. We represented a point on the triangle with barycentric coordinates. By setting this equal to the ray equation, we solve for the distance \(t\) and two barycentric weights using dot and cross products. If the ray is parallel to the triangle, we reject it. Otherwise we check whether all three weights are nonnegative and whether \(t\) is inside the ray's range. We also use the barycentric weights to interpolate the three vertex normals and get the normal at the intersection point.

Spheres. For the sphere intersection we used the sphere equation with center \(\mathbf{o}\) and radius \(R\). Substituting the ray into \(\lVert \mathbf{p}-\mathbf{o}\rVert^2-R^2=0\) gives a quadratic equation \(at^2+bt+c=0\) where \(a=D\cdot D\), \(b=2(O-\mathbf{o})\cdot D\) and \(c=\lVert O-\mathbf{o}\rVert^2-R^2\). If the discriminant is negative there is no intersection. Otherwise we first check the smaller root \(t_1\). If it is before the minimum \(t\) we check the larger root \(t_2\) instead. This also handles the case where the ray starts inside the sphere. We still check that the chosen root is in range. For the normal we take the normalized vector from the sphere center to the hit point.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

BVH construction

For the BVH a recursive construction was used. We first get the range of primitives for the current node and calculate a bounding box that contains all of them. If the number of primitives is small enough we make a leaf node and stop splitting. Otherwise we split them into two groups.

To split them we calculate a bounding box of the primitive centroids. We choose the longest axis of this box and use its midpoint as the split position. Primitives are split according to which side of the midpoint their centroids are on. We then rearrange the primitives so the two groups are together and recursively build the two child nodes.

One edge case we considered is when all the centroids are at the same position. This would fail to split the primitives so we just divide the range into two halves by the number of primitives instead.

Splitting heuristic. We chose the longest axis of the centroid bounding box because this is where the primitives are spread out the most. Splitting across this direction can reduce overlap between the two child boxes, so it is easier to skip one of them when tracing a ray. We used the centroid box instead of the whole primitive bounding box because one large triangle could make that box much bigger, even when most of the other triangles are close together.

We also chose this method because it is simple and does not require sorting. We only go through the primitives to find the split, and the BVH for Lucy's 133,796 triangles took about 0.04 seconds to build. Other methods such as a median split or surface area heuristic might make a better tree, but our method worked well enough for the scenes we tested.

BVH traversal

Bounding box test. We used the slab method to check whether a ray intersects a bounding box. For each of the x, y and z axes, we calculate the two \(t\) values where the ray crosses the box planes, using \(t=(\text{plane}-O_a)/D_a\). We precompute \(1/D_a\), and swap the two values if the ray is traveling in the negative direction. We then take the latest entry time and earliest exit time over all three axes, while also checking the valid ray interval. If the entry is after the exit, the ray misses the box.

Traversal. Starting from the root we check if the ray intersects the node's bounding box. If it does not we skip that node. If the node is a leaf we check the primitives stored inside it. Otherwise we recursively check both children. Whenever we find a closer primitive we update the ray's maximum \(t\). This way we can skip boxes that are farther away than the closest hit found so far. For shadow rays we return once we find any intersection since we only need to know whether something blocks the light.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

Direct lighting

For this part we used the diffuse BSDF which returns the reflectance divided by \(\pi\). For zero bounce lighting we return the emission of the surface the camera ray hits so it only shows the area light. Two methods were implemented to estimate the direct light at a hit point and both use Monte Carlo to estimate the reflection equation:

\[ L_{out} \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \frac{f(w_j \to w_o)\, L_{in}(w_j)\, \cos\theta_j}{p(w_j)} \]

Uniform hemisphere sampling. Here we pick random directions uniformly in the hemisphere around the normal so every direction has the same pdf of \(1/(2\pi)\). The number of samples is the number of lights times the samples per area light. For each direction we shoot a ray from the hit point which starts slightly away from the surface so it does not hit itself. If this ray hits something we take the object's emission. We multiply it with the BSDF and \(\cos\theta\) and divide by the pdf before averaging across all the samples.

Light sampling. Instead of hoping a random direction hits a light, this method samples the lights directly. A point light only needs one sample since every sample would be the same, while an area light gets the full number of samples per area light. Each sample gives us the direction to the light, its distance, its pdf and the incoming radiance. If the light is behind the surface we skip it. Otherwise we cast a shadow ray toward the light that stops just short of it, and if nothing blocks it we add the radiance times the BSDF and \(\cos\theta\) divided by the pdf. We average the samples for each light and sum over all lights. This also means point lights work, which random hemisphere directions would basically never hit.

Part 4: Global Illumination

Indirect lighting

For indirect lighting we need to pick a new direction each time a ray hits a diffuse surface. We used cosine weighted hemisphere sampling so directions near the normal are more likely, giving a pdf of \(\cos\theta/\pi\).

The bounces are handled recursively. The camera ray starts with the maximum ray depth, which drops by one at every bounce. At each hit we add the direct lighting from Part 3. If there is depth left we sample a new direction from the BSDF, trace a ray from slightly off the surface and weight what comes back:

\[ L_{out} \mathrel{+}= \frac{f(w_i \to w_o)\, L_{in}\, \cos\theta_i}{p(w_i)\, p_{cont}}. \]

To stop rays from bouncing forever, we used Russian roulette with a continuation probability of 0.65. We always allow the first indirect bounce if the maximum depth is greater than 1. After that, each bounce has a 65% chance of continuing. When a path continues, we divide its contribution by 0.65 to account for the paths that were stopped. This lets us avoid tracing too many bounces without making the result systematically darker.

The final pixel color is the emission at the first hit (zero bounce light) plus the result of the recursion. We also added an option to show only the \(m\)th bounce, where we only add direct lighting at that bounce and show just emission for \(m=0\).

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

Implementation

For Part 5, we implemented adaptive sampling so that we don't have to take the maximum number of samples for every pixel. Some pixels need fewer samples to get a stable result, while others are still noisy after many samples. Instead of always sampling up to the maximum, we check whether each pixel has converged after every batch and stop sampling it once it meets the tolerance. To do this, we store the sum of the sample brightness values \(x_k\) and the sum of their squares, \(s_1=\sum x_k\) and \(s_2=\sum x_k^2\). After each batch, we calculate the mean, variance and confidence interval:

\[ \mu = \frac{s_1}{n}, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n - 1}\left(s_2 - \frac{s_1^2}{n}\right), \qquad I = 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}. \]

Here \(I\) is half the width of the 95% confidence interval. We check if \(I\) is smaller than the tolerance multiplied by the mean \(\mu\). If so, we consider the pixel converged and stop taking more samples. Otherwise we continue until the maximum sample count. The final pixel color is still calculated by averaging the samples actually taken, and we record that number for the sample rate image. We also clamp the variance to zero if floating point error makes it slightly negative, so taking the square root does not cause an issue.

Results

We tested adaptive sampling on the bunny and spheres at 480×360. We set a maximum of 2048 samples per pixel, batch size 64, tolerance 0.05, 1 light ray and max ray depth 5. In the sample rate images, red areas are pixels that needed almost all 2048 samples, while blue areas converged with fewer samples.

Rendered image Sample rate

From the sample rate images, we can see that the darker parts of the scenes generally need more samples. For example, the shadows under the bunny and spheres and the darker sides of the objects are mostly red, meaning they used close to the maximum of 2048 samples. The light source itself converges much earlier, while some areas around the ceiling also need a lot of samples.

One reason is that pixels receiving indirect lighting can have more variation between samples. Our stopping condition also depends on the mean brightness of the pixel, so darker pixels need a smaller confidence interval before they can stop. For the timings, the bunny took 24.5 seconds and the spheres took 33.8 seconds using 14 threads.
